Here's a fact that surprises almost everyone: a crypto wallet doesn't hold any crypto. Your coins never sit inside your phone, your browser or a little USB stick. They live on the blockchain, a shared record copied across thousands of computers around the world.

So what's in the wallet? A key: the secret that proves the coins are yours, and the only thing that can move them. Lose the key and the coins are still there, but nobody can ever touch them again. Leak the key and someone else can spend them, from anywhere in the world, in seconds.

That turns the most important question in crypto into a surprisingly simple one: who holds your key, and how is it protected? I'll answer it by following one person's 500 dollars from an exchange into a wallet of his own, step by step. There's a full video version below if you'd rather watch.

Meet Bilal

Bilal is a freelance developer. A client in Canada paid him 500 dollars in USDC, a dollar stablecoin (I explained how that payment works in my stablecoins explainer). Right now those 500 USDC sit in his account on a crypto exchange, which means the exchange holds the keys, not Bilal.

He decides to move the money into a wallet of his own. We'll watch every step: creating the wallet, backing it up, receiving the money, sending some to his brother, and getting it all back when his phone ends up in a river. (Bilal is a teaching example, but every step is exactly what real wallets do.)

Three ideas first

The blockchain. A shared record book, copied across thousands of independent computers called nodes. Every copy says the same thing: which address owns how much of which coin. Nobody can quietly edit it, because every other copy would disagree. Moving money on a blockchain doesn't mean moving files. It means adding a new line to that shared record.

Public and private keys. Crypto uses a pair of mathematically linked numbers. The private key is a secret number. From it, the wallet works out a public key, and from that a shorter address. The maths only runs one way: anyone can go from the private key to the address, but nobody can go backwards. Think of the address as a glass mailbox with your name on it. Anyone can see it and drop money in. The private key is the only key that opens it.

Signatures. To send money, your wallet uses the private key to produce a digital signature for that exact payment. Anyone can check it against your address and confirm the owner approved it, but the signature doesn't reveal the key and can't be reused for a different payment. It's a cheque signed with a pen nobody else can copy, where the signature only fits that one cheque.

The one-sentence version

A crypto wallet is a tool that creates, stores and uses your private keys, so you can prove you own coins on a blockchain and sign payments that move them.

A better analogy than a purse is a car key. The car (your coins) stays parked in a public car park (the blockchain), where everyone can see it. The key doesn't contain the car, but whoever holds the key can drive it away. That's why the crypto saying goes: "Not your keys, not your coins."

Step 1: Create the wallet and the seed phrase

Bilal installs a self-custody wallet app and taps "Create new wallet". The app picks a huge random number. In the most common standard, BIP-39, it's 128 bits long: about 3.4 × 10³⁸ possibilities, a number thirty-nine digits long. Nobody can guess it, even with every computer on Earth trying for longer than the age of the universe.

Humans can't remember a 128-bit number, so the app turns it into words. It adds a small 4-bit checksum (a built-in typo detector), splits the 132 bits into twelve 11-bit chunks, and uses each chunk to pick one word from a fixed list of exactly 2,048 words (2¹¹ = 2,048). The result is Bilal's seed phrase, also called a recovery phrase: twelve ordinary English words in a specific order. A 24-word phrase works the same way with 256 bits.

From those twelve words, the app derives Bilal's private key, then his public key, then his address, which in our example starts with 0x8f3a and ends with c21e. The same twelve words always produce the same key and the same address, in any compatible wallet app.

Step 2: Back it up, offline

The app shows Bilal his twelve words and asks him to write them down. This is the most important moment in the whole process. He writes them on paper, in order, double-checks them, and keeps the paper somewhere safe. No screenshot, no notes app, no email to himself, no cloud backup.

Why so careful? Because the seed phrase isn't just one key. It's the master copy. Modern wallets are "hierarchical deterministic" (the standard is BIP-32): from one seed they can generate a practically endless tree of keys and addresses, for many accounts on many blockchains. Whoever has the words has everything.

Some wallets also let you add an optional passphrase on top of the twelve words, sometimes called the "25th word". It creates a completely separate set of wallets, so someone who finds only the paper still can't reach the money. Forget the passphrase, though, and nobody can recover it.

Step 3: Receive

Bilal copies his new address into the withdrawal screen on his exchange. He sends a small test amount first, waits until it shows up, and only then sends the rest. That habit costs a few cents and protects against a wrong address, because blockchain payments can't be reversed.

Here's the key insight: nothing travels into his phone. When the withdrawal goes through, the blockchain adds a record saying that address 0x8f3a…c21e now owns 500 USDC. Bilal's wallet app simply reads the public blockchain and displays that balance. If he looked up the same address on a block explorer like Etherscan, he'd see the same 500 USDC there.

Step 4: Sign and send

A week later, Bilal's brother needs 100 dollars. Bilal types the amount and his brother's address, and taps "Send". Here's what happens:

  1. The wallet builds the payment: "Send 100 USDC from 0x8f3a…c21e to this address."
  2. Inside the phone, it signs that exact payment with Bilal's private key.
  3. It sends only the signed payment to the network. The private key itself never leaves the phone.
  4. Thousands of nodes check the signature against Bilal's address. It's valid, so the payment goes into a block. (Curious what those nodes actually do? Here's my explainer on blockchain nodes.)
  5. The shared record now says: 100 USDC to his brother, 400 USDC left at Bilal's address.

One practical catch: every blockchain charges a small network fee (on Ethereum it's called gas), and it's paid in the network's own coin, not in USDC. So Bilal keeps a few dollars of that coin in the same wallet. Forgetting it is one of the most common beginner surprises: a wallet full of stablecoins that can't move. (My gas fees explainer covers why the fee exists and what sets its price.)

Step 5: Restore

A month later, Bilal's phone slips off a bridge into a river. Is his money gone? No. He buys a new phone, installs a wallet app (it doesn't even need to be the same brand, as long as it supports BIP-39), chooses "Restore wallet", and types his twelve words in order. The app derives the same key and the same address, reads the blockchain, and shows 400 USDC. Everything is back, because none of it ever lived on the phone.

Now flip that around. Anyone who gets those twelve words can do exactly the same, from anywhere, without his phone, his password or his face. That's both the beauty and the danger of a seed phrase.

The whole journey in one line:

Random number → 12 words → private key → address → sign → restore.

Hot or cold, custodial or self-custody

Every wallet answers two questions, which gives four boxes.

Hot or cold? A hot wallet is connected to the internet: a phone app or a browser extension. It's convenient for everyday payments and DeFi apps, but it's exposed to malware, phishing and fake websites. A cold wallet keeps the key offline and only signs when you deliberately use it.

Who holds the key? With a custodial wallet, such as an exchange account, the company holds the keys and you hold a login. With self-custody, you hold the keys, backed up by your seed phrase.

Hardware wallets. For bigger amounts, a hardware wallet is the strongest option for most people. It's a small device built for one job. The private key is created inside its secure chip and never leaves it. When Bilal sends money, his laptop prepares the payment, but the device signs it. It shows the amount and the destination on its own small screen, and nothing happens until he presses a button on the device.

Even a laptop full of malware can't extract that key. It can, however, try to trick Bilal into approving a bad payment, which is why reading that small screen matters.

Why not just leave it on an exchange?

Custodial accounts are easy, and if you forget your password, support can reset it. But you're trusting the company with your coins, and history has punished that trust:

  • Mt. Gox, 2014. Once the world's largest bitcoin exchange, it collapsed after losing about 850,000 bitcoin. Creditors waited about a decade for partial repayment.
  • FTX, November 2022. After a rush of withdrawal requests, FTX froze withdrawals and filed for bankruptcy, with a shortfall reported at about 8 billion dollars. Most customers were eventually repaid around 118–119% of their claims from 2025, but valued at November 2022 prices, so they missed the large price rises in between.

Self-custody removes that company risk, but it cuts both ways. There's no support desk and no reset button. Lose the device and the twelve words together and the coins are gone for good. Nobody can recover them, not even the company that made the wallet app. You become your own bank, and your own security team.

How people actually lose their coins

Most losses aren't genius hackers breaking cryptography. People are tricked. Chainalysis counted about 158,000 personal wallet compromises in 2025, affecting at least 80,000 victims, with about 713 million dollars stolen from individuals. The usual tricks:

  • Fake support. Someone posing as wallet or exchange support asks you to "verify" your seed phrase. Real support never asks for it. Ever.
  • Fake websites and apps. A look-alike site or a fake app-store listing asks you to type your twelve words to "restore" or "sync" your wallet.
  • Leaked backups. A photo of the seed phrase sits in a cloud photo backup or a notes app and gets exposed.
  • Malicious approvals. A pop-up asks you to approve a token permission for a "free airdrop". The approval quietly lets a drainer contract take your tokens.
  • Address poisoning. Scammers send you a tiny payment from an address that looks almost identical to one you've used, with the same first and last few characters, hoping you'll copy it from your history next time. In December 2025, one trader lost about 50 million dollars in USDT this way.

Even professionals get fooled. In February 2025, the exchange Bybit lost about 1.5 billion dollars in ether, the largest crypto theft ever recorded. The attackers, whom the FBI attributed to North Korea's Lazarus Group, had tampered with the web interface of the multi-signature wallet service Bybit used. Staff saw what looked like a routine transfer and approved it, but the transaction they signed handed control of the wallet to the attackers.

The lesson applies to everyone: check every address, and read what you sign, ideally on a hardware wallet's own screen.

Three myths

"My coins are stored in my phone (or my hardware wallet)." They're recorded on the blockchain. The device holds the key that controls them.

"If I lose my seed phrase, someone can reset it." No one can: not the wallet app, not the blockchain, not a support agent. That's the price of nobody being able to freeze or take your coins.

"A hardware wallet makes me unhackable." It protects the key from malware, but it can't stop you from approving a bad transaction or typing your seed phrase into a fake website.

The honest limits

  • Irreversible mistakes. A payment to the wrong address can't be undone. Test payments and address books help.
  • A single point of failure. A plain seed phrase is one secret. Fire, flood or theft of the paper can mean total loss or total theft. Some people use metal backups or split their backup across locations.
  • Inheritance. If only you know where the words are, your family may never be able to reach the funds.
  • Usability. Twelve words, gas fees and long hexadecimal addresses are still a big hurdle for newcomers, which is exactly what the next generation of wallets is trying to fix.

A note from practice

From building smart contracts and dApps: the most dangerous button in a hot wallet isn't "Send". It's "Approve". A token approval lets a contract spend your tokens later, often with no upper limit, and it stays active until you revoke it. Many drains happen weeks after a careless approval.

So check the amount and the contract when you approve, prefer exact-amount approvals, and every so often review and revoke old approvals with a token-approval checker. And keep a separate "burner" wallet with small amounts for trying new apps, while your savings sit in a wallet that never connects to anything new.

Wallets are getting friendlier

These are trends, not finished fixes:

  • Smart wallets (account abstraction). Your account becomes a small program with rules: daily spending limits, several approvers, or social recovery, where trusted devices or friends can help you regain access. Ethereum's standard for this is ERC-4337.
  • EIP-7702. Ethereum's Pectra upgrade, live since 7 May 2025, lets ordinary accounts take on smart-wallet features such as batching several actions into one and paying fees in other tokens. More than 11,000 accounts used it in its first week. The original private key still has full control, so protecting it still matters.
  • Passkeys and MPC wallets. Some wallets now sign you in with a passkey (your phone's fingerprint or face unlock) or split the key into pieces held by different parties (multi-party computation), so there's no single twelve-word phrase to lose.

Five rules for keeping your crypto

  1. Write your seed phrase on paper or steel, and keep it offline.
  2. Never type it into a website, and never share it with anyone.
  3. Keep savings in cold storage, and only spending money in a hot wallet.
  4. Send a small test first, and check the whole address.
  5. Read what you sign, every time.

And one more, because it's how most people lose money: nobody legitimate will ever ask for your seed phrase. Not your exchange, not a wallet company, not a "support agent" in your DMs, and not me.

Your wallet doesn't hold your coins. It holds the key that moves them.
